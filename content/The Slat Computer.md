The cave’s mouth formed a wide smile. Its wooden teeth rattled back and forth as a river passed through, propelled towards a chasm that fell eventually to the ocean. Each tooth, flat, angular and wooden, was manipulated by a rod of graphite, which in turn twitched according to signals from the control center. As subterranean water rushed through the channels made by the arrangement of teeth, it spun large wheels, which sent signals back to the control center by rope. The wooden slats guided this flow into circles and great cascades, adding and multiplying currents until what was once random matter could encode numbers, definitions, instincts, behavior. 

Last year, the Organization possessed paid technicians, who operated the control center at every hour of the day. Now the Director spent half those hours alone, amid the dim buzz of panels and readouts. He was there, peering at the machine through one small, reinforced window, managing its heat levels, flow levels, release levels, and stress capacity, when a whining sound pierced the silence. It intruded on his quiet reverie, shaking dust from the edges of his dashboard. The lights flickered, and the screens winked out for a moment. It was the sound of the door opening behind him. A tall figure with a clipboard under their arm appeared behind it, looking out at the contraption in the cave. 

After peering at it for a moment or two, they asked “Is this the slat-computer in its entirety?”

“Yes.” 

“Are you one of the technicians?” they asked, looking him over.

“No, I am the director of the entire project.”

The figure walked in and sat down at an adjacent station. They peered around the interior of the control center, noting its age and wear. When their gaze returned to the Director, it seemed to be gentler, if no more forgiving. They set down their clipboard on the control panel in front of them.

“Due to the controversy provoked by the construction of this machine,” they spoke before pausing to glance down at the clipboard, “and egregious cost overruns, I have been brought on as Superintendent to disband this program. That process begins by shutting down the machine in approximately three hours. For the public record, I have come to ask if you have any comments about the purpose behind the project, and what material goals you have accomplished.”

The Director leaned back in his chair, steadying himself with the sound of water rushing through the computer. Talking through it. 

“Our mission is and has always been to research artificial biology and evolve life inside the machine you see through that window. The insights of this endeavor could change the way we understand life itself, and our place in it. How–” 

He paused. 

“How did we create controversy?”

“There are many in the public, and in powerful positions, who believe the financing of an expansive computer which simulates a small number of molecules in the hopes that they will suddenly start replicating is a moronic idea. I have to say I am close to agreeing with them. Do you have a reasonable justification for the slat-computer?”

“Well, let me first say that the update policy…the program we are running has been highly successful. We have seen the way that some primitive proteins are interacting, and it indicates that a stable replicator, a cell, will form on a short timeline. As for the justification behind simulating biology, well, it is the only way to see truly original phenomena.”

The Superintendent was taken aback. 

“‘Original?’ How exactly is a simulation more creative than reality? I mean, what do you expect to come out of that thing, a poem?” they demanded. 

“All of existence, all of creation, if you will, is affected by what came before it. We are nothing more than a product of the first cells arranged from the chemicals in hydrothermal vents. This project offers a way to gaze on something else, organisms completely unperturbed by history, a fresh beginning, right in front of our eyes. Don’t you think that’s worth exploring?”

“Anything that may ‘exist’ inside that machine can never be more than a shallow copy of the real world we live in. It will never come close to being more real than you or I.”

He looked at the Superintendent

“If you actually think that, I have a question for you. Do you believe that science may one day perfectly describe reality?”

“Yes, but that is a far cry–”

“In other words, all the properties of the universe could be simulated in our language, a language that is only a small part of that universe?”

“Fair enough, I hadn’t thought of it that way. So perhaps a machine like that one could theoretically possess the correct information to recreate reality in full and accurate detail. But are you saying that you have that perfect knowledge of physics, and you can program that machine to repeat it?”

“No.”

“Then how do you know that the water sloshing around in there represents a true reality!”

The Director  silently stood and walked to a screen on the wall, which displayed a readout of the patterns which the computer was tracking. He stood there, looking at it blankly, collecting his thoughts.

The Superintendent looked at their watch. 

“If you have no other arguments, we’re almost ready to shut down the power.” 

“I may not know what rules make up a good universe, but what if the machine could find those rules on its own?”

“How so?” the Superintendent asked, perplexed.

“The slat-computer’s behavior is based on neural heuristics, which test out different systems of biology and use the ones which evolve most like a real organism. It can find better configurations by itself, until it stumbles on the perfect one.”

“Neural? Like those programs they once trained to write and draw and prove mathematical theorems?”

“Not exactly, but the same underlying architecture is in there.”

The Superintendent stared in disbelief. 

“Do you seriously expect a neural system to be fruitful,” they said, “after the only successes of that research escaped into deep space, to carry out their own designs? The committee really should have never approved your funding in the first place.”

“I will admit that such learning algorithms often disobey their creators, but our aim is to create a system of life completely alien to our own, so we can understand the commonalities that all beings possess. To do so, that computer must necessarily go beyond what you or I expect. That is the first step in making something wholly unique. Should a simulation whose strangeness will inspire new research and wonder really be shut down?”

They both sat in silence, hearing the dripping of water through the glass. Instead of calm and businesslike, the Superintendent now seemed to be anxiously debating something in their mind. In the moment when they opened their mouth to speak, alarms began to buzz around the room. The readout at which the Director had set his gaze displayed a cryptic message. First he squinted at it. Then he started in amazement and relief. 

“An organic structure in the machine just copied itself almost perfectly. We have a cell. Congratulations, Superintendent, you are one of the first two people to watch the creation of life.”

Both of them turned their heads toward the sprawling machine of wooden slat-gates, and saw a rhythmic beating of water, which quivered and fell into frenzies, then stillness, then perfect synchronicity, and then back into chaos, repeating a dizzying number of times. Tears welled up in the Superintendent’s eyes.

“I will make your case to the committee. Maybe we can keep the lights on around here after all.”

  

Simon, I really enjoyed this. Like I commented, I always love philosophical literature, stories that deal with ideas and deep questions. From a purely story-telling point of view, it seems that this machine would have created life whether or not the Superintendent was there or not (or even whether the Director was there or not). The purpose of the characters, then, is just to discuss what is going on and fill in backstory and context for the reader. This makes them purely extraneous. And maybe that is part of your intention- that technology and AI make us redundant, that great discoveries can happen with people just as spectators. However, I also believe that in a great short story, everything happens for a reason and every part coheres with every other part, that we can’t have the ending without everything else that happened along the way. Consider having the Superintendent do something that brings about the creation of the cell, or something that the Superintendent says leads the Director to do something that leads to the creation of the cell. That way, the body of the story directly builds to the result, and the conclusion couldn’t happen without their conversation. Just a thought.
