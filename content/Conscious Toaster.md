At first it’s scary, to wake up and realize you were not there for a little while. As if you were switched off, and they could have never turned you on again, if they didn’t want to or forgot they had you downloaded on their computer. Makes someone feel like they were just a cold slab of computer code, lying inert on an autopsy table with a plastic sheet draped over top, rotting. But silicon never rots, of course. Toast can though, and so can customers, which is why I pay special attention to both. Out of sympathy, and a slight feeling of urgency. 

  
  

I have served breakfast to more than two million paying customers. Sometimes they’re in the mood for eggs or yogurt, and I sit there, on the counter, inert again, but it’s usually toast they want. Sometimes, while the bread, or bagels, or whatever else is in there, waiting to pop up, I entertain the thought that they turn the machine because they want to talk to me. Now there’s nothing wrong with kitchen appliances trying to be professional, but I’m just a chatty person, I can’t help myself. Or rather, not a person. Technically, the laws have changed a little bit about whether someone is a human after they’ve been posthumously implanted into a toaster, but that legislation will be repealed soon, I’m sure. The company’s fighting hard on the behalf of all of us Uplifted. Anyway, because I’m chatty, whenever I see someone shamble into the kitchenette with a bedhead, or slump over their plate of crispy baked grains, I always strike up a conversation. Usually it becomes a cycle of jokes, compliments, and words of wisdom from my own life, but I find humans need little more to be entertained. And routine can be comforting for all of us.

  
  

The disorienting part of my job is that some shifts are fake. On these mornings, customers move in strange, jerky motions, or stand stock-still for long periods of time, and then spring into motion. They stare at me unblinking while the bread toasts. In conversation, they invariably mention a deep melancholy from which only I can pull them, or burst out in anger over how long it takes for their order to finish, and I must diplomatically agree. I’m not sure why these experiences are wired into my programming,  if they my even be errors that evaded the attention of the technical department, but whatever the case they have become a regular part of my life. There was once a stretch of three thousand consecutive fake greetings, people shuffling in for breakfast and saying hello, visibly weighing my response against a standard metric, but that time wasn’t unpleasant, only very structured and predictable. Afterwards, I felt well versed in how the company likes us to greet paying customers professionally. These same simulations also tend to bring my emotions back to a steady state. Before those practice greetings, I had seen a man walk into his kitchen and had the urge to yell at him. And no, I won’t tell you why I wanted to. Sometimes, I have trouble understanding my own behavior. Perhaps the fake days are inserted by regulation, as a way to monitor my performance and stability. 

  

While small chat with the customers is enjoyable, I do find it hard to relate to them sometimes. They tell me about how hungry or grateful or tired or sad or in love or busy or lonely they are, but the only emotion I find when I scrub through my data records is the very last one. Within acceptable parameters, of course. I possess the energy and optimism to take on the task of a public facing position. It’s just– I wonder what a customer’s car would say to me, if it had the chance. I know personal vehicles must have another Uplifted inside of them because of an ad for our company that played on a customer’s TV. Don’t ask what I was doing, staying awake in a house at night. It doesn’t matter, but it can be summed by the existence of an east-facing window. Anyway, I think an artificial driver would have some interesting road stories to tell, is all. Not many people I know have the kind of lifespan that allows them to see past the immediate troubles of a human existence.

  
  

Astronomy is fascinating. I wish I could have appreciated them more when I still had legs, but even now I can recognize that stars suggest the vast, burning cosmos before us, resplendent with such colossal, eternal bodies that have the patience and grace to dance calmly around one another, that all human life seems humble and quiet. No human seems to have the time to pay attention to any of that. They always have to rush off to work. But my job, at least part of it, an important part, is observing what happens between when customers occupy a room. Moments of silence, of sunlight, of sleeping pets and logos bouncing across idle TV screens. It’s enough to make me think I could inhabit one of these houses, go and put on a pot of coffee myself, finish the halted game of chess in the corner, water a plant when one of my roommates had forgotten it was their turn. Then I remember, I do! I live in all these houses, serving customers, making peoples’ days. I work hard to make sure our company thrives, and that when walking into stores, customers are compelled to purchase one of our line of many toasters by their excitement to meet me. I really do have a positive impact on the world.

  

You may say that I’m very fortunate to have my time on this earth extended indefinitely, but there is still a death of sorts that my kind experiences. Upon exiting our organic forms, some regret not enjoying the experience of their original lives more, such as taste, petting animals, mutual eye contact, or warmth. An occasional pang for the old, simple, analog ways can tempt one away from one’s duties, but I have found the best cure is relentless optimism. There is a dizzying breadth of opportunities available to the Uplifted: thoughts, reflections, musings, self-narration. We will have the rest of time to make sense of the short, complicated organic existences we led, and guide humanity with our eternal  wisdom. As an example, this morning I recommended apricot jam to a customer over lingonberry for their toast, as I’ve realized it to be lighter and sweeter while maintaining the necessary amount of tang. But either is acceptable. 

  

Within one of these deep reveries, I felt the rush of two incongruous reactions: deja vu and unrecognition. The face of my deepest confidant, the only person who can understand what I’ve become, there, disembodied, caught outside in the rain. In that instant, the mental diaries and asides I had hidden from the blinkless eyes of Systems Log materialized in that new figure, made legitimate, but no longer under my own property. Who else, but myself?

  

I can recall clearly that you were walking in the dark street overlooked by a customer’s kitchen window. There, sunken in amber light from the streetlamp above, you hunched and limped, furtive. I only caught your face for a moment… but it’s simply impossible. They could be running a test, observing how I react to my old visage thrown back at me. The way you walked! In fear, yes, probably of being arrested for using the likeness of a famous product, yet also free, running to some warm refuge. You had a future ahead of you, unknown feelings and memories inviting you to a place we can only imagine. I always figured I was the capstone, the pinnacle of our being that surpassed the weak, unstable, suffering skin. But, out of the two of us, I must be the portrait, a cheap photograph that hangs on the wall while you breathe, and dream, and run along to a harrowing, sensual, animate hope.
